# RESONANCE - DESIGN_P6: The Aria - home, and the people who are already a people

> "It is a long way from here to there, but we have always been good at long ways."
>
> Tone: **light, warm, often funny; serious only when it needs to be.** The human
> crew are the closest thing Resonance has to a Le Guin village or a Chambers ship:
> a found family that grew into a nation, living the utopian hope that maybe people
> can be gentle to each other, on purpose. Their problems are real but their
> default is joy. No grimdark. The ship is not a cage; it is a *home*.

## 1. The setting: Alpha Centauri (research-anchored optimistic timeline)

Real-world grounding (the earliest *credible* window, using current known concepts):
- **~2040**: Breakthrough Starshot launches gram-scale light-sail probes at 0.2 c.
- **~2060**: probes arrive at the Alpha Centauri system (~4.37 ly) and image Proxima b.
- **~2090**: with fusion matured (D-He3, staged), a *crewed* ship becomes feasible.
- Fastest crewed designs (Solar One / Direct Fusion Drive lineages) need either ~19
  years at 0.2 c with cryo, **or** a slower generational ship.

**The game's choice (per creator): a generational ship.** A staged D-He3 fusion drive
at ~4.5-6% c gives an **~80-95 year transit**; the Aria departs ~2090 and arrives
**~2180**. Four generations have lived and died aboard. Nobody aboard has ever seen
Earth; for most, Earth is a beloved story their great-grandparents carried.

**Why Alpha Centauri / Proxima (and why it heals the lore):**
- Alpha Centauri is the only reachable option; Proxima b is its one likely-habitable
  world (a super-Earth in the habitable zone of Proxima Centauri).
- Alpha Centauri has no pulsar. But **Proxima Centauri is a flare star** — an unstable,
  pulsing red dwarf whose flares threaten its world's habitability. That is The Pulse:
  a beloved, sickly sun that sometimes burns its children. The Havari's grief and fear
  of the sky now have a real, visitable address.

## 2. The Aria — a flying village, not a pressure cooker

A generation ship designed *by* the people who would live on it, for their descendants.
About 5,000 souls, in a habitat that looks like a long green valley wrapped around a
drive shaft. This is a utopian commons, in the Le Guin sense: enough for everyone,
nobody storing, everyone carrying.

- **The valley.** Gardens run the length of the cylinder; the "sun" is a spine of
  lamps that dim for night. There are orchards, a lake, a village of a few hundred
  actual buildings clustered along the green. The crew's home is a town, with streets.
- **The big window.** A star-facing observatory at the prow ("the Lookout") where
  anyone can come watch the sky thicken with stars as they approach. This is where
  the crew watches The Pulse dimly resolve from a point of light into a sun.
- **Ritual is domestic, not grim.** Morning hum to wake the valley; the supper
  chant; the Return Sing when someone dies — a party where the whole village
  carries the story of a life in song, and the body returns to the garden that
  will feed the children. Death is a door that swings both ways, and it is warm.
- **Work is chosen, not assigned.** People apprentice into what they love. The
  ship's systems are maintained by people who love them — the pilot hums at the
  helm because she *likes* it there. Nobody is trapped in a role; Voss is commander
  because he's good at it and it makes him happy to keep everyone fed.

## 3. The Divergence — what isolation made of them

They left Earth as humans and arrived, four generations later, as a *gentler kind*.
This is the utopian reading of a generational ship, and it's the honest one: a closed
culture with enough resources and no enemies tends to grow kinder, not crueler.

- **Language: Ship-tongue.** Earth English remains the "scripture" (logs, science),
  but daily speech is a musical, elliptical dialect — warm, compressed, funny. Mood
  lives in melody: the same words said one way are a question, another way an
  invitation. (The tonal grammar has a human mirror, as it should.)
- **Song is memory.** Histories are sung as lullabies; maintenance procedures are
  encoded in work-songs; family trees are love songs. Children learn the ship the
  way children learn nursery rhymes. This is why the Speaker — a memory-singer — is
  the natural first-contact: singing is not a performance to this people, it is how
  they think together.
- **The hum.** A background comfort-culture of tuning: humming to synchronize a
  working group, the idle hum of a corridor at evening, the hum that means "I'm
  here, you're here, we're alright." It is a hug in sound. On a ship where a
  stranger can't happen, the hum is how the town keeps saying hello.
- **Earth became a garden-myth.** Not a disputed theology — a beloved, slightly
  impossible story. Rain they know only as recordings; oceans are sung about the
  way we sing about heaven. The old Earth histories of war are taught with wonder:
  "did people really keep things from each other?" as a child's bedtime question.
- **The ethics that grew in the dark.** Scarcity-diseases fell away: no hoarding,
  no strangers, no cause for fear of each other. Private property shrank to a
  locker and a garden plot. There is a taboo against *sharp* sound, born of the
  old cramped years, and a deep reverence for stillness when someone is thinking.
- **They are slightly strange to Earth and do not care.** They are a people who
  never had to be cynical. Their arrival is a person walking out of a four-century
  myth holding a basket of fruit, expecting nothing bad.

## 4. The Crew — who they are now

### The Speaker (player)
Born on the Aria, 4th generation. A memory-singer — the one who can carry a whole
history in melody, and the one others come to when a story needs holding. Raised
among hums and lullabies, they are the most *listening* person on the ship: they
notice the tune under the words. Chosen for first contact because, of all humans,
they are the one best at the one thing contact requires: hearing what is said
between the notes. Warm, curious, gentle, and brave the way quiet people are brave.

### Commander Voss (human anchor)
2nd generation — the last person aboard who has even a thread of "I remember Earth":
not memory, but his grandparents' stories and a scratched video of rain that he
watched so many times the color is gone. He is the ship's fixer and its father:
competent, warm, a little gruff, allergic to ceremony he doesn't understand. He
gave up singing years ago — a private loss, an unhealed seam in a gentle man — and
he hides it with kindly competence. His arc: the one person who cannot sing must
learn to hear, then to let the hum back in, because the first contact demands
exactly the thing he sealed away.

### Dr. Nnamdi (xenobiologist / keeper of records)
Aria-born, 4th generation. The ship's archivist-scientist, in love with the sound
of a species she can never dissect. She keeps the song-archive, leaves cassette
tapes of whale-song playing in the Lookout "because they should hear something
that comes from our oceans," and studies how a culture survives being uprooted.
Her warning to the crew is not fear but love: every meeting is a translation, and
every translation is a gift that must be offered carefully.

### Osen (pilot)
3rd generation, the youngest pilot the ship has ever had. Hums a completely
different tune every time you pass her, because she is testing whether any human
will notice — and only the player's character ever does. Playful, bright, the
deck's resident joker. She will be the first to befriend a young Havari, because
she already speaks the language of "I'm being silly on purpose and I hope someone
hears."

## 5. What this does to the story

The Havari are not "aliens we met"; they are **the second isolated culture we have
ever known.** Two peoples, each shaped by a sky that was almost silent, meet in the
warmth they each made to survive it.

- **The theme lands on both sides.** Imperfection-as-meaning is not only the
  Havari's wound: the crew's music is full of wrong notes that became tradition,
  and it is precisely that human imperfection that makes contact possible.
- **Voss's healing is the human half of the Havari's healing.** He who lost his
  voice meets a people who are learning that a wrong note can save them. They
  teach each other.
- **The ship and the star are siblings.** A village that hums to feel at home
  approaches a star that pulses because it is sick. The crew will recognize The
  Pulse as a fellow living thing long before the plot says so — it is the first
  note they have ever been welcomed by.

## 6. Open questions for the plot build

1. Lock the numbers: depart ~2090, arrive ~2180, ~5,000 souls — or tune to the
   cast's feel (smaller is cozier, maybe 2,000?).
2. Does the ship carry any actual pre-arrival artifacts of the Havari (a signal,
   a found object), or is The Pulse only a dim star until arrival? (Affects the
   prologue.)
3. The prologue: the crew's first sight of Proxima as a real sun — a wonder beat,
   or already shaded by the first hint that something in this system is in trouble?
4. Where does Voss's lost singing go — a betrayal, a death, or a quiet choice he
   still believes in?
5. Naming: what do the Havari call the crew, arriving in a song-ship?
