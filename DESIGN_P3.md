# RESONANCE — Design Bible (Part 3: Craft Guides, Thrael, The Pulse)

## 4.6 Craft Guides — what we borrow from Le Guin and Chambers

These are *working rules* we keep beside us while writing every scene, line, and
character. They are not homage — they are craft.

### From Ursula K. Le Guin — "the alien is a full person, and translation is sacred"

1. **The Alien is Not Us, and Not a Mirror.** Le Guin's Athsheans (Word for World is
   Forest) and Gethenians (Left Hand of Darkness) are not "humans with different hats."
   Their difference is *constitutive*: it changes the grammar of their morality, their
   politics, their love. Our Havari must be the same — the reader/player should never be
   able to fully "solve" them into humanity. Seravak, Ilyan, Thrael each hold one
   assumption that simply *does not translate*, and we never force a tidy reconciliation.

2. **Translation is a Moral Act.** Genly Ai fails because he translates Gethenian terms
   into his own categories ("king," "country") and so never sees Estraven until it's
   nearly too late. The Speaker's arc is literally built on this: every time she
   translates a Havari phrase into a *human* frame too quickly, she loses something; every
   time she lets the phrase stay alien, she gains trust. The game's translation UI will
   sometimes offer TWO translations and let the player choose — one "accurate to the
   human ear," one "true to the Havari voice" — and the consequences are real.

3. **Communication as Vulnerability.** In Le Guin, dialogue is risk: to speak to an alien
   is to *offer yourself to be misunderstood*, and misunderstanding can kill (it almost
   kills Genly Ai). Our Echo/Dialogue mechanic makes the player's *actual voice* the
   vulnerable thing. You literally cannot complete a conversation without offering your
   imperfect, breakable voice. That is Le Guin's "the word for world is forest" — you
   must go into the forest unarmed, singing.

4. **The World is Not the Plot.** Le Guin's worlds are *bigger* than the conflict.
   There is weather, there are meals, there is a sense that life continues outside the
   frame. Our hub (the observation deck / the Havari garden) will have ambient, non-plot
   moments: Ilyan humming a nonsense song, the Pulse's light shifting with the tide, a
   word that exists only in poetry. These are not filler; they are the *world*, and they
   make the plot's stakes feel real. (This is also deeply Chambers.)

5. **Silence as Speech.** Le Guin knows that what is not said is the loudest thing.
   The Havari's only lie is silence — so the most important "dialogue" in the game may be
   the moment Seravak *stops singing* in the middle of a phrase. We will not let the
   player skip over silence; the game holds it, the music drops, and the pause itself is
   the line.

### From Becky Chambers — "depth lives in the small, patient, everyday"

1. **The Boring Scenes Are the Story.** Chambers (Long Way to a Small Angry Planet, the
   Monk & Robot books) builds love and trust through *shared maintenance*: making tea,
   fixing a door, sitting together in silence. The Resonance hub will include "nothing"
   interactions — tending the garden with Ilyan, sharing a meal with Voss, humming along
   with the Pulse at dawn — that award no score and advance no plot, but *do* raise
   trust and unlock hidden character moments. The player who speeds past them will
   literally miss most of the game's soul.

2. **Kindness is Plot, Not Flavor.** In Chambers, a character choosing to be kind at
   cost to themselves IS the dramatic climax (Tupo's death, Ouloo's hospitality, Dex's
   decision to stay). We will treat the player's small choices — choosing to listen, to
   stay, to sing for someone who asked for nothing — as the true branching points, and
   the "missions" as the scaffolding that lets those choices happen.

3. **Every Species is Many People.** Chambers never makes a species a monolith; her
   Aandrisks and Humanoid Crew are full of individuals who disagree with their own
   culture. Our Havari are not one psychology. Seravak, Ilyan, and Thrael are three
   *rival* interpretations of what it means to be Havari — they fight each other as much
   as the plot. The culture is a debate, not a datasheet.

4. **Food, Sleep, and Rhythm.** Chambers grounds every alien in bodily life. So do we:
   the Havari rest by singing in circles; they "eat" by letting resonant light play on
   their skin; their courtship is a call-and-response that never ends. These details are
   shown, not told, and they make the aliens feel *alive* rather than conceptual.

5. **No Evil, Only Mismatched Needs.** Chambers has no villains, only people whose needs
   collide. Thrael is written exactly this way: his harm to the player is real, but it
   is born of grief and doctrine, not malice. The game's final beat should make the
   player *understand* Thrael so completely that "beating" him feels wrong — you don't
   defeat Thrael, you *finish his phrase for him.*

## 4.7 THRAEL — the Negotiator (violet, metallic, exact) ★ THE THIRD SURPRISE

**Surface:** the stern diplomat; the suspicious official; the antagonist who demands
accuracy and threatens to sever contact. The player expects the uptight bureaucrat.

**The surprise:** Thrael is *not* suspicious of you — Thrael is **afraid of hope.**
Thrael has spent a lifetime maintaining the *orthodoxy*: the belief that the star's
dying is the Havari's own failure of harmony, that they must be *more perfect* to be
saved. This doctrine is the culture's only comfort. If you (an imperfect alien) can
communicate and *help*, the doctrine is proven wrong — and with it, **a thousand years
of grief-ritual is revealed as a wasted vigil.** Thrael's enmity is the enmity of a
grieving parent protecting a sacred lie.

**Deeper still:** the game's **lie-detection mechanic** (§6) reveals the truth before
Thrael ever says it — Thrael's "official" phrases are sung *microscopically out of tune*.
The player *hears* the negotiator's private pain in every official phrase and can call
them on it. When finally broken open, Thrael sings — for the first time in their life —
a genuinely dissonant, childish, *hopeful* phrase, and it saves the contact. Thrael's
arc: **the disciplinarian learns to allow themselves to be imperfect.**

**Norm-breaking core:** *the enemy is not a villain; she is the most despairing faithful
person you have ever met, and her weapon is ritualized, heartbreaking hope.*

### 4.8 THE PULSE — the Star (deep blue, vast, slow) ★ THE FOURTH SURPRISE

**Surface:** the mythic force; the god-voice; the thing everyone *sings to*. The player
expects a cosmic deity who bestows wisdom.

**The surprise:** The Pulse is not a god. The Pulse is a **symbiote — and it is lonely
the way a mountain is lonely.** The star's "pulse" is literally its breathing; the Havari
evolved to *resonate with it*. When the Havari were young and dissonant and *felt
everything loudly*, the star sang back, and their two songs **interfered constructively** —
a mutual amplification that was, biologically and astronomically, a relationship.

**The tragedy:** the Havari perfected themselves into consonance, became calm and
beautiful and *harmonious with each other* — and in doing so **stopped resonating with
the star.** The star has been slowly going quiet, not from age, but from *hunger for a
voice that disagrees with it.* It does not need worship; it needs **an unanswered
phrase** — a song that challenges its fundamental note. That is what the Speaker, with
her imperfect, defiant, human voice, can finally give it.

**Norm-breaking core:** *the god needs you not to pray to it, but to sing to it as an
equal — to finish its phrase with a note it could not have predicted. The final act of
the game is not worship; it is a duet.*

### 4.9 COMMANDER VOSS — the human anchor (steel grey, spoken-word only)

**Surface:** the pragmatic captain; the scientist; the one who says "report to the
bridge." The player expects the mission-control voice.

**The surprise:** Voss is the human who most resembles a Havari — because Voss has
**almost lost the ability to sing.** He was once a musician; a war and a loss took his
voice, and he has built his life on *measurement, not feeling*, as a defense. He is the
character the Havari find *most* alien, because he is the least tonal — and the player
slowly realizes that the true first contact is not the Speaker meeting the Havari, but
**Voss learning to hear again.** His arc culminates in the finale, where — in the
silence, with no instrument — Voss hums a single broken note into the dark, and it is
the bravest sound in the game.

**Norm-breaking core (for the human side):** *the human foil is the one who must become
more alien — more open, more resonant, less defended — while the aliens learn to become
more human. The two species meet in the middle and are changed by it.*
