# RESONANCE — Design Bible (Part 4: Plot, Mechanics, Aesthetics)

---

## 5. The Two Mini-Games as Story Machines

The mini-games are not "activities" — they are the *proof* of the story. Each one
translates a theme into an action the player physically performs with their voice.

### 5.1 MINI-GAME 1 — THE ECHO: "prove you heard me"

**What it is:** The NPC sings a phrase. You listen. You sing it back. The game analyzes
your pitch against theirs and scores *fidelity*.

**Story meaning:** Echo is **trust as accuracy.** Every successful Echo is the Havari
thinking *"you heard me, therefore you might be real."* Every failure is *"you heard me
and did not care to reproduce me — you are a noise, not a voice."* The Havari do not
care if you can *say* the words; they care if you can *become* the words. Echo is
demonstrating that you are willing to be *used* by another being's phrase.

**The lie-detection mechanic (THE heart of the whole game):**
Because a Havari physically cannot lie, the game's scoring engine — normally measuring
*player* accuracy — is secretly also measuring the **NPC's** accuracy against their own
canonical phrase. Thrael's phrases are programmed to be sung **14-22 cents flat** on the
final note: a microscopic, *audible-to-the-ear* crack in the perfect surface. If the
player Echoes Thrael *exactly* (including the flatness), Thrael is furious — you have
"caught" her. If the player Echoes the phrase *as it should be* (correcting the flatness),
Thrael is also shaken — you have shown her what honesty sounds like. There is no safe
answer; there is only *awareness*. The player who notices the flatness has discovered the
game's deepest secret before any character tells them.

**Difficulty curve = intimacy.** Early phrases are slow, wide-interval, forgiving (trust
is easy). Late phrases are fast, microtonal, demanding (trust is earned). The final Echo
of the game is the *child's phrase* — the simplest melody in the entire game, deliberately
easy, because by then the point is no longer accuracy; it is *willingness to sing a
child's song in front of the whole galaxy.*

### 5.2 MINI-GAME 2 — THE DIALOGUE: "answer me as an equal"

**What it is:** The NPC sings a **Form** (a statement, a question, a challenge). You must
*respond* with a phrase from your learned library — not the same phrase, but the *right*
phrase. Judged on grammar (interval relationship to their Form), not rote.

**Story meaning:** Dialogue is **trust as understanding.** The Havari don't need you to
repeat them; they need you to *know what they meant and answer that meaning.* A question
(rising contour) must be answered with a *confirmation* (falling to their home note). A
challenge (tritone) must be answered with a *refusal* (tritone slide away) or a
*submission* (falling fifth). Getting it wrong is not a "game over" — it is a *social
error* that changes the relationship. The Havari forgive being echoed badly; they do not
forgive being *misread.*

**Mood system = the Le Guin translation choice:** Dialogue answers are sometimes offered
as *two true options* — one "accurate to meaning," one "true to feeling." Both are
grammatically correct. One builds trust with Thrael; one builds trust with Ilyan. The
player learns that translation is a choice with consequences, exactly as Genly Ai learns
on Gethen.

## 6. Story Structure (six movements, mapped to gameplay)

> Structure follows the Havari sense of time — a cycle, not a line. The game is shaped
> like a musical form: it opens and closes with the *same* phrase, transformed.

**MOVEMENT I — "A Foreign Kindness" (tutorial, trust ≈ 0→15)**
The Speaker wakes on the observation deck of the *Aria* (the vessel). Contact has just
been made. Seravak sings the first phrase — the greeting — and teaches you the Echo. Voss
watches, skeptical. First surprise: Seravak laughs at your terrible singing — *delightedly,
not kindly.* She teaches you a deliberately wrong phrase to see what you'll do.

**MOVEMENT II — "The Child's Argument" (trust ≈ 15→40)**
Ilyan finds you, sings the child's melody, and asks (in a rising contour) a question that
means both "who are you" and "are you a risk." You learn the Dialogue. Ilyan introduces
you to the youth choir — the "dissonants." Second surprise: you realize Ilyan is not a
child at all; the *choir* is a political movement, and you are its newest argument.

**MOVEMENT III — "The Perfect Lie" (trust ≈ 40→60)**
Thrael formally opens negotiations. This is where the lie-detection mechanic bites: the
player must Echo Thrael's *perfect* official phrases and feel the *flatness.* Voss
dismisses it as audio error. The player knows it isn't. Third surprise: Thrael's
"official" harmony is a lifetime of sung grief. (Optional: the player can choose to
*point out the flatness*, winning a breathtaking confession — or to let it pass, which
Thrael notices and never forgets.)

**MOVEMENT IV — "The Unresolved Dissonance" (trust ≈ 60→80)**
Ilyan breaks. They sing the *unresolved dissonance* — the second taboo — in front of you
and cannot stop. The game's music grinds to a halt. This is the Chambers "boring scene"
that is actually the climax: the player has no mission, no score, only the option to
*hold the silence with them.* (The silence IS the answer.) Ilyan, exhausted, teaches you
their true name.

**MOVEMENT V — "The False Accusation" (trust ≈ 80→90)**
The star's pulse drops below the threshold Havari call "the line." Thrael publicly sings
the accusation — that the humans brought the stillness. Voss orders evacuation. Fourth
surprise: **The Pulse itself answers.** It sings a phrase no Havari has heard in a
millennium — *an unanswered question.* The star is not dying of age; it is starving for
a second voice. The player now understands: the doctrine that has comforted the Havari
for a thousand years is a lie *by omission* — the culture sang only to itself, perfectly,
and forgot there was someone else to sing *to.*

**MOVEMENT VI — "The Duet" (trust ≈ 90→100)**
The finale. The entire Havari choir assembles; the Pulse breathes its slow phrase. The
player must sing the *answer* — the phrase that completes the star's question. It is the
child's phrase, transformed by everything learned: not a repetition, but a *response.*
As you sing, the Echo and Dialogue mechanics fuse — you must both *reproduce* (the
choir's harmony) and *respond* (the star's question) at once. If you succeed, the star
sings again, and the Havari weep because the world is *in tune.* Thrael, in a single
vulnerable phrase, thanks you. Voss hums his broken note into the silence. The final
line, sung over the credits in the player's own recorded voice: *"We are here. We were
always here. We were just listening."*

**Endings (three, all earned by trust, not by winning):**
- **The Chorus (high trust + high Dialogue skill):** you stay, and become a permanent
  voice — the game's "true" ending. The last screen is the Player singing the child's
  phrase with the whole choir, on a loop, forever.
- **The Voyage (high trust, lower skill):** you leave with Voss, but the melody goes
  with you; the credits play over a recording of the *Aria* drifting, the Havari singing
  it home. Bittersweet, gentle.
- **The Silence (any trust, if you never truly listened):** the star's question goes
  unanswered. The ending is quiet, and the player is left holding the phrase they chose
  not to sing. This is not a "failure" — it is the game trusting the player to know what
  they did.

## 7. Aesthetics — every pixel serves the alien

**Visual language:** no human faces, no "aliens with foreheads." The Havari are shown as
*choir-forms*: flowing, bioluminescent bodies that *move the way their music sounds* —
their mantle pulses with the phrase, their bladders glow per note, their whole body is a
visualization of their own voice. When they speak, you don't read subtitles so much as
watch the *shape* of their meaning. The UI itself is diegetic: the "word" for a phrase is
drawn as a *ribbon of light* (its contour), never as text. Even the player's own HUD
translates their singing into the same ribbon language — so the game's interface is, on
purpose, *learning to read the alien's script.*

**Sound:** procedural. Every alien has a timbre (Seravak: warm sine with slow AM — like a
didgeridoo made of light; Ilyan: bright, slightly sharp, with a wobble like a young bird;
Thrael: metallic, with a glassy sheen — the voice of someone who has filed themselves
down; The Pulse: a vast, slow, sub-bass pulse with a barely-there high partial that
sounds almost *sad*). The soundtrack is built from the same chords the Havari sing — the
player is literally *inside* the alien language, all game, and only notices at the end.

**The one human image in the whole game:** the credits. Everything alien and beautiful
behind us, the camera turns to the Speaker's hands on a ship's console — and we hear, for
the first and only time, *real human singing*, the player's own recorded voice from the
finale, unprocessed and imperfect. It is the whole game in one shot: the imperfection
that saved them.

## 8. System Notes (kept minimal — systems serve story)

- **Two scorelines:** *Harmony* (Echo accuracy) and *Truth* (Dialogue understanding).
  The game displays them as the player's two "chords." Neither is a win condition; both
  gate *character* content, not plot.
- **Trust** is per-character and non-linear; it unlocks the Chambers "boring scenes,"
  which are the only way to learn the deepest phrases (Seravak's humility phrase, Ilyan's
  true name, Thrael's child's phrase).
- **No fail states in the usual sense.** A failed Echo lowers trust and changes dialogue,
  but never ends the game. The only true "failure" is disengagement — and the game is
  gentle enough to hold that too.
