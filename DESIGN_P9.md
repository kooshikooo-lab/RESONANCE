# RESONANCE - DESIGN_P9: the mini-game - Meridian, the city, and the Collegium

> "The city was built where day and night meet, because that is where everyone
> was willing to stand."

Frame: this is the **mini-game** (see UNIVERSE.md §6). Its soul is the singing
theme - the Havari's tonal language, the player's broken voice, and the small brave
act of being imperfect on purpose. Its body is a **college in a diplomatic city**:
an intergalactic hub built for diplomacy and exchange, where every species on the
nearest stars is trying to learn everyone else's language, cuisine, manners, and
music - and mostly succeeding, hilariously.

---

## 1. The city: Meridian

A city has to be somewhere. Ours is on the **terminator of a tidally locked world**
orbiting Proxima - the narrow band of eternal twilight between a dim red day that
never ends and a night that never ends. Two reasons: the day side is bathed in the
flare star's tantrums (you need shutters, not curtains), and the night side is
permanent dark (you need lamps, not windows). So they built the city *on the line*,
stretching like a ribbon around the whole world, with towers that lean into both.

- **The sky:** to the day, a low, heavy, coppery-red sun that never moves. To the
  night, a black hung with stars and the pale smear of Earth's own sun - a thing
  the Havari call "the little day." The horizon glows where the light grazes the
  curve. When Proxima flares - which it does, often, on no schedule - the whole
  day sky washes orange for hours and every city shutter slams shut at once, which
  is a sound the residents know as well as a heartbeat.
- **Why it exists:** diplomacy and exchange. The city is the meeting point of the
  two star systems - the Aria's humans and the Havari, plus whoever else came to
  watch. Its reason for being is that peoples who live four years of light apart
  need somewhere to *talk*, and Meridian is where talking happens, on purpose,
  forever. Embassies, exchange markets, language bars, art bazaars, and one very
  strange college.
- **The ribbon shape:** the city is long, not tall or round - a continuous strip of
  districts running along the twilight line. The spine is the **Exchange**, a grand
  open concourse where all the embassies face each other across a canal of water
  that freezes on the night side and steams on the day side. Everything else hangs
  off the Exchange like beads.
- **The modesty problem (worldbuilding comedy, played warm):** nobody here agrees
  on what it's rude to show. To one species, baring your forearms is an insult. To
  another, wearing anything at all is faintly baffling. Meridian's etiquette code
  is a living, laughing document, and the worst sin in the city is *not* a bare
  body - it's a cultural assumption. The city's unofficial motto: "Point with your
  whole hand, and nobody takes offense."

### 1.1 Districts (working set)

- **The Exchange** - the spine. Embassies, the market, the news.
- **The Gardens** - farms and parks on the twilight line, where crops grow under
  red sun on one side and lamps on the other. Half the food is grown here; the
  other half is imported and fought over.
- **The Foundry** - industry and maintenance. Where the city repairs itself.
- **The Collegium** - the college quarter (below).
- **The Docks** - where ships (few, slow, precious) come and go over decades.
- **The Quarter** - housing, theaters, bars, the long night-market under the
  never-ending night side. The city's living room.

## 2. The Collegium: the college

**Meridian Collegium** - the college of the city, dedicated to the only thing the
city is for: **learning to be understood, and to understand.** It is not a
military academy or a trade school; it is an exchange of persons. Half the
students are diplomats' children, half are the diplomats themselves retraining,
half are support-crew kids and hangers-on who just wanted to be here - which is,
as every college is, three halves.

The Collegium's pride is the **School of Voice**: the study of the Havari tonal
language and of singing as a *diplomatic medium*. Because in this universe, the
species whose language is tone has made voice the center of exchange - you cannot
translate a people who sing by writing notes at them. Voice is the bridge, so
Voice is the school.

- **The student body:** all the city's peoples (see §4). The choir is mixed-
  species, which is a sonic chaos and a civic treasure.
- **The faculty:** a handful of humans who can sing a little, a few Havari who
  teach tone the way humans teach grammar, and one legendary Chirr who teaches
  "improvisation" (which is to say, unlicensed maintenance of the choir loft).
- **The classes are the tutorial.** This is the mini-game's smartest trick: the
  mechanics are diegetic. *Tone theory* teaches the player to read emotion-as-
  contour (the pitch-reading game). *Xenolinguistics* teaches the no-lies rule by
  having the player hear when a Havari's tone wavers (the listening game). *Choir
  practice* teaches the pitch-matching game with forty aliens who are all, to a
  one, better than the player. *The player, whose singing is broken, enrolled
  anyway* - and that is the game.
- **The arc (thematic, not a locked plot):** the player is the only one in the
  choir who sings badly, and they keep singing badly, on purpose, because it is
  theirs. The Havari, raised to flawless performance, eventually have to decide
  what to make of a person who is imperfect *by choice* and proud of it. The mini-
  game is about that collision, in the warmest possible register.

---

## 3. The player

A new student at the Collegium. Ordinary - not a chosen one. They enrolled in the
School of Voice with a voice that can't hold a tune, which their friends consider
either very brave or very funny (it is both). They carry a private, amateur habit
of singing - badly, happily, to themselves - that has nothing to do with anyone
and might, in the end, matter to someone anyway.

Working notes, not locked: the player is a diplomat's kid, or a dockworker's kid,
or neither; the point is that they chose Voice despite having no gift for it.
Working name: unneeded yet - "the player" is fine.

## 4. The peoples of Meridian (cultures, clothing, and the lack thereof)

A rich city is a crowd. Each people is a *people* - coherent, ordinary-to-
themselves, never exoticized (see DESIGN_P8 §3 and DESIGN.md §2 for the rule). A
note on clothing: this universe treats bodies and garments as *matter of fact*,
each species dressed (or not) for its own reasons, none of them mystical.

### 4.1 The Aria's humans
Post-scarcity, post-privacy, radically direct. They wear **soft, patched,
pocketed clothes** - the Aria's culture mends rather than replaces, and a garment
with visible repairs is a garment with a history, worn proudly. Layers, warm
colors, deep pockets full of seeds and tools. They find the city's etiquette wars
hilarious and are the ones who write the city's jokes about it.

### 4.2 The Havari
The locals. Bioluminescent floaters with a tonal language; their skin glows with
their mood, so **they wear almost nothing, and what they wear is expression, not
modesty** - woven sashes and stoles of thin membrane, dyed and strung with tiny
resonant beads that rattle in harmony as they move. A Havari "outfit" is a
*statement about the wearer's current song*: bright and clattering for joy,
muted and beadless for grief. They find human modesty quietly touching and faintly
odd, like watching someone apologize for having ears.

### 4.3 The Vessk (radial, chitin-shelled)
Hard-shelled, patient, and permanent. Their clothing is **armor that is also
memory**: plates of shed shell, engraved with records, worn over softer inner
robes. A Vessk's wardrobe is their family archive - you can read three generations
of their history on their shoulders if you know the script. They dress slowly,
deliberately, and consider human fashion *alarmingly temporary*.

### 4.4 The Umlou (slow, soft, gravid)
They move like warm weather and dress like it too: **enormous, heavy, flowing
robes** that wrap them in layers of weight and warmth and slowness. An Umlou
taking a seat is a ceremony. Their robes are embroidered with constellations
- their only ornament, and it is for remembering, not for show.

### 4.5 The Chirr (small, fast, many-limbed)
They arrived with nothing and dress accordingly: **harness vests of recycled
everything**, every strap a pocket, every buckle a tool, every patch a story about
something they took apart. They shed and re-layer constantly. Their clothing looks
like a disassembled machine that decided to take a walk - and it basically is.

### 4.6 The Borei (suited, from a cold world)
They come from a world so cold that a bare body is an emergency. Their **climate
suits are the body they actually wear**: seamless, personal, expressive - the
suit's color and pattern drift with mood and weather. Being suitless is intimate
to them, like nakedness to a human, and it is *exactly* that serious and that
ordinary. They think the Umlou's robes are beautiful and worry about them getting
cold.

### 4.7 The Neth (textile-speakers)
Their language is woven. The Neth **speak in fabric**: garments whose patterns
shift as they converse, sleeves that "say" the polite part, hems that "say" the
honest part. To read a Neth you must be able to read cloth, and their tailors are
their poets. They find everyone else's clothing *devastatingly loud* and can't
understand how humans manage to say so little with so much fabric.

### 4.8 The Ember (light-painted)
Their art is on themselves. The Ember **paint their skin with slow-burning
light-pigments** and wear no fabric at all - their bodies are their canvases and
their modesty is irrelevant because they have never had any, not because they
rebelled against it. An Ember in formal dress is an Ember painted all over in
shifting constellations. They are the city's best decorators, night-market
owners, and worst-dressed people (if you consider nothing clothing, which they
do not).

### 4.9 The Pyre (feathered, unclothed)
Warm-blooded, feathered, from a temperate world - **they wear nothing and find
human clothes faintly ridiculous**, like wrapping a bird in a cocoon. Their
display is their feathers: color, molt, and grooming tell the whole story. A Pyre
"dressing up" is preening until you notice. They are cheerful about everyone
else's costumes and will absolutely ask you to explain your trousers.

The crowd rule: in Meridian, **everyone is dressed (or not) for a reason, and no
one's reason is wrong.** The comedy is never at a culture's expense; it is at
everyone's, together, gently.

## 5. Architecture: the weird, grounded

Meridian's architecture is "all sorts of weird" because it is *built by everyone
for themselves*, side by side:

- **Havari quarters** - built for ears: no hard corners, curved and vaulted
  surfaces, whispering galleries, open choir-pavilions. Walls that resonate and
  streets that hum to themselves. To a human it feels like walking inside a
  song; to a Havari it feels like standing still.
- **Human quarters** - the Aria built for efficiency and repair: straight, safe,
  mended, every surface either a garden, a window-box, or a visible patch.
  Towers lean toward the day side for the red sun's light.
- **Vessk towers** - stone on stone, engraved, built to outlast the builders'
  grandchildren. The oldest structures in the city; the Vessk are still adding
  to them.
- **Borei halls** - sealed, climate-perfect, fogged glass that frosts into
  patterns when you pass.
- **The Exchange** - the city's cathedral of meetings: one long open colonnade
  of all the species' columns mixed together, roofed in a shimmer of every
  material at once, lit by day-red on one side and by lamps on the other.
- **The flare-shutters:** every building in Meridian has them - great, soft,
  silvery screens that slam down over every window when Proxima flares. When
  they drop, the city's whole character changes for hours: muffled, amber,
  intimate. Children count flares the way Earth children count rainstorms.

## 6. The singing theme, in the mini-game

This is the mini-game's reason to exist (UNIVERSE.md §6). Concretely:

- **The player's broken voice is the mechanic and the meaning.** They read the
  Havari's emotion-as-contour, they match pitches in choir practice, they hear
  when a tone wavers (the no-lies rule) - and they do all of it while being the
  worst singer in the room, on purpose. The game never fixes the player's voice.
  The game *is* the player's voice, imperfect, and that is the point.
- **Classes are the tutorial** (§2) - tone theory, xenolinguistics, choir.
- **The choir is the world.** A mixed-species choir of every people in §4, all
  better than the player, all slowly realizing that the player's off-key joy is
  not a failure - it is the thing none of them can do.

## 6.5 Meridian and the Outpost (two cities, one universe)

The main game's setting (DESIGN_P7) is the **Outpost** - a young, frontier
diplomatic station, "interplanetary college" energy, first-days-of-a-new-town.
The mini-game's setting is **Meridian** - a rich, mature, dense city, the old hub.

They are not the same place, and that is fine and useful:
- **The Outpost** is where the main game's ordinary crew live: new arrivals,
  not yet unpacked, everything still being built.
- **Meridian** is the older sister city - the grown-up hub of exchange, with
  centuries of architecture layered in. The Collegium's oldest faculty remember
  when Meridian was a frontier outpost too, and tell stories about it that the
  main-game's residents would recognize.
- Both are on Proxima's world; Meridian is the old capital, the Outpost is the
  new suburb. Characters can, and should, travel between them - the universe is
  small on purpose (UNIVERSE.md §3).

## 7. Open questions (mini-game)

1. **Who is the player, exactly?** Diplomat's kid, dock kid, or neither - does it
   matter? (Lean: neither, deliberately - ordinary, like the main-game player.)
2. **What does the player *do* besides sing?** Errands across the districts?
   Helping the choir? The class-tutorial structure gives the shape; the "do"
   needs one more idea.
3. **How big is the mini-game?** A few hours of vignettes, or something loopier
   (an endlessly replayable choir/language game)? (Lean: vignettes first - it
   matches the universe's anthology nature.)
4. **Does the main-game player exist in the mini-game?** Same person, or a
   different ordinary voice? (Open - the universe allows both.)
5. **The Havari arc:** how explicitly does the mini-game touch the "flawless
   performance is smothering them" theme - full arc, or one quiet scene?
   (Lean: one quiet scene.)
