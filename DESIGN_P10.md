# RESONANCE - DESIGN_P10: the mini-game - FlirtLearn, or the courtship method

> "Nobody has ever failed to learn a language they wanted to say something in."

Frame: this is the second mini-game (see UNIVERSE.md §6). P9 is the singing theme
(Havari tonal language, the broken voice). **This one is the courtship method** - a
language-learning game where the player is a student at the Collegium learning a
*human* language (Spanish, French, Japanese...), and **flirting is the curriculum**.
The cast is aliens and humans mixed; you pick your character, you pick your target
language, and you learn it by trying to charm someone in it.

It is warm, it is funny, and it is quietly real: people actually do learn languages
faster when they want to say something. The game just makes that the whole point.

---

## 1. The premise: the courtship method

The Collegium's School of Voice teaches Havari to humans through song (P9). Its
sibling - the **Language Exchange** - does the reverse and wider: it teaches *human*
languages to anyone, and alien languages to anyone, and it uses the oldest trick in
teaching: **motive.** You don't memorize; you *want*. You learn the word because you
need it tonight.

- Officially the Exchange calls it "motivational immersion." The students call it
  **FlirtLearn**, and the name is not a joke - it is the method.
- Every student gets a **partner** (a native speaker, or a more advanced student, of
  their target language) and the assignment is the same for everyone: *ask that
  person out in your target language. All semester.* The grammar, the vocabulary,
  the pronunciation - all of it is built out of that one assignment.
- It works. It works so well that the Exchange has the highest fluency rate in the
  Collegium, which the faculty mention with the exact air of people who accidentally
  invented something wonderful and are now responsible for it.

## 2. The characters: aliens among humans, and you pick

The roster is **aliens and humans together** - the Exchange mixes them on purpose.
You choose one character to play. Each has a home species, a target language, a
personality, and one person they want to say something to.

### 2.1 The player characters (working roster)

- **A Havari** studying Spanish. (Their own language is tonal; Spanish's *stress*
  and rolling *r* feel strange and physical to them, and they sing every sentence
  out of habit. They are learning Spanish because someone on the Exchange said
  "buenas noches" in a way that rearranged them.)
- **A human** studying Japanese. (A dock kid who fell for a Neth who weaves their
  sleeve in characters. Now they need to learn how to read the sleeve.)
- **A Chirr** studying French. (They want to be able to complain about the Exchange's
  coffee with the precise, exquisite vocabulary it deserves. Also, someone.)
- **An Ember** studying Russian. (Their light-pigments say what their mouth can't
  yet; they need the words to match the glow.)
- **A Pyre** studying Mandarin. (They learned the tones are the whole game - a
  language that is half melody - and they want to hear a real conversation in it,
  and be in one.)
- **A Borei** studying Spanish. (Their climate suit translates; they want to not
  need the suit. They are learning to flirt in the open air.)

Each character is a *choice* among human and alien faces on the same screen - the
game never separates "the aliens" from "the humans." They are just students. The
player can be any of them.

### 2.2 The partners (the crushes, the "teachers")

Every player character has a partner - who is simultaneously the object of the
assignment, the conversation partner, and often the de-facto tutor. These are
drawn from the same mixed roster:

- **A human from Earth** (a recent arrival, fluent in the target language, patiently
  charmed by your mistakes).
- **A Havari** who is learning *your* language at the same time - so you are each
  other's teacher, which is the Exchange's favorite arrangement.
- **A Vessk** who is rigorous and delighted, and corrects your grammar with the
  gentle authority of someone whose family history is engraved on their shell.
- **A Neth** whose sleeve is doing half the flirting for them, while their voice
  stays perfectly, devastatingly calm.
- **A Umlou** who speaks slowly and warmly and makes you slow down too.
- **A Chirr** who learned three pickup lines before learning the word for "hello,"
  and is extremely proud of this.

Partners are not prizes to be won by correct grammar. They are people with their
own reasons for being there, their own mistakes (they are also learning!), and
their own arcs. You are both beginners at something.

## 3. The mechanics: flirting IS the study

The loop is simple and it never lets you forget you are in class:

1. **You have a phrasebook, not a dialogue tree.** Your available lines are the
   words you have actually learned. A beginner has "hola," "¿cómo estás?," "me
   llamo..." and one very hopeful "¿te gusta...?" Everything else is closed until
   you learn it. This is the whole trick: **your language level and your love
   level rise together**, because the way to get the next line is to be brave
   enough to use the current one.
2. **Flirting is output practice.** Saying "me gustas mucho" in real time - to a
   real (written) person who is watching your grammar - is the highest-stakes
   language practice there is. Mistakes are not penalized into failure; they are
   the point. You will mispronounce "llamo." They will find it endearing. The
   game is built to make being bad at a new language *lovable*, not embarrassing.
3. **Your partner teaches.** When you guess wrong, they correct you - gently, with
   the real-world explanation ("actually you'd say 'te quiero' to someone you
   love, 'te gustas' is more like... here, watch me say it"). These corrections
   become permanent phrases in your book. **Your romance is literally your
   textbook.**
4. **Levels are semesters.** Each tier of the relationship (meeting, small talk,
   a real question, a confession, whatever happens next) is a "semester" with its
   vocabulary set. You cannot skip ahead by grinding drills; you advance by using
   the language to move the relationship forward. Fluency and intimacy are the
   same stat.
5. **The teacher-student theme is explored in both directions.** Sometimes your
   partner is ahead of you and you are the student. Sometimes *you* are ahead -
   your partner is learning your language and you get to be the teacher, which is
   its own kind of tenderness. And sometimes the "teacher" is an actual teacher:
   a faculty member who is also a student of another language, because at the
   Exchange everyone is both. The game deliberately asks: **what does it mean to
   teach someone something? What does it mean to be taught? And when you are
   falling in love with the person teaching you, which of you is really learning?**

## 4. Real languages, real words (the grounded core)

Per DESIGN_P8's rule - no magic, everything real - FlirtLearn uses **actual human
languages with actual words.** Spanish is the default example (the user's own), and
the roster above sketches French, Japanese, Russian, and Mandarin. The phrasebook
is a real curriculum:

- **Semester 1 (meeting):** greetings, names, "where are you from," one genuine
  compliment.
- **Semester 2 (small talk):** likes and dislikes, plans, "want to grab a drink?"
- **Semester 3 (something real):** "I keep thinking about you," the difference
  between *te quiero* and *te amo*, how to apologize in the language.
- **Semester 4 (the brave part):** whatever the character and partner earn.

The player genuinely learns these words - the game is a cozy, romantic language
primer wrapped in a love story. That is not a side effect. It is the point. (If it
teaches you to order coffee or say something true to someone in Spanish, the game
has done its job in the real world, which is the most utopian thing a game can do.)

**A note on the teacher-student theme, done right:** the utopian register means
power is handled honestly. The "teacher" is never an authority exploiting a
student; the teacher is a *peer ahead* - a tutor, an exchange partner, or a
faculty member who is openly, hilariously also a beginner at something else. The
game's answer to the theme is that **everyone at the Exchange is both student and
teacher**, and the people you learn from are the people you are learning *about*.

## 5. The Exchange, the place (shared with P9's Collegium)

Same world as DESIGN_P9: Meridian, the Collegium, the ribbons of districts, the
flare-shutters, the nine peoples. The Language Exchange is a wing of the Collegium,
built as a **garden of small rooms** - study alcoves, language cafés, a huge open
courtyard where the paired students practice out loud and everyone pretends not to
listen (everyone listens; it is the Exchange's favorite sport).

- **The language café:** where partners meet. Menus in six scripts; the barista
  (a Chirr) knows which table is learning what and keeps the coffee coming.
- **The phrase wall:** a wall where students write the best line they learned that
  week in their target language, and everyone else translates it into their own.
  The wall is covered, floor to ceiling, in tiny love letters in every language in
  the city.
- **The faculty room:** three humans, two Havari, one Vessk, and a Neth who argues
  in their sleeve. They are, against all odds, a happy family.

## 6. Open questions

1. **Which languages, how many?** Spanish, French, Japanese, Russian, Mandarin as
   the sketched five - or fewer, done deeper? (Lean: start with one real language
   - Spanish - and make it *complete*, then add.)
2. **The teacher-student beat:** how explicit should the faculty-student romance
   be? (Lean: the romance is peer-to-peer; the faculty member's arc is *being a
   beginner at another language*, which keeps the power honest and the theme rich.)
3. **Replayability:** does each character/partner pair have a distinct story, or
   one story with interchangeable cast? (Lean: distinct - it is the universe's
   anthology nature.)
4. **How much actual language teaching?** Vocabulary drills and grammar nudges
   embedded in the flirt, or full lessons? (Lean: embedded - the game teaches by
   *wanting to say something*, not by drills.)
5. **Rated how?** The register is warm and sweet, not explicit - a love story, not
   a conquest. Confirm this is the tone.
