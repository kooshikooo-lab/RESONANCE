# RESONANCE - DESIGN_P10: FlirtLearn - the courtship method

> "Nobody ever failed to learn a language they wanted to say something in."

Frame: the second mini-game (UNIVERSE.md §6). P9 is the singing game. This one is a
language-learning game set in the Collegium's Language Exchange. You are a student
learning a real human language (Spanish first). The method: you learn it by trying
to charm one particular person in it. The cast is aliens and humans together; you
pick your character, pick your target language, and spend the semester trying to
say one true thing.

Warm, funny, and honest about wanting. Erotic in the broad sense - desire, skin,
touch, kissing - and never pornographic. The game goes to the bedroom door and
stops, always.

---

## 1. The premise: the courtship method

The Language Exchange teaches human languages to anyone (and alien languages to
anyone), and it uses the oldest trick in teaching: motive. You do not memorize;
you want. You learn the word because you need it tonight.

- The Exchange calls it "motivational immersion." Students call it FlirtLearn.
- Every student gets a partner - a native speaker, or a more advanced student, of
  their target language - and one assignment: ask that person out in the target
  language, all semester. Grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation all come out of that
  single task. The exam is a date.
- It works, and it works so well that the Exchange has the highest fluency rate in
  the Collegium, which the faculty discuss with the air of people who accidentally
  invented fire and are now responsible for it.
- Why it works: a language is a thing you do with your body - lips, breath, hands.
  You cannot say "I want you" in a new mouth without feeling the shape of the want.
  Desire is not a distraction from learning. It is the fastest learner in the room.

## 2. Gameplay styles (the question to explore)

FlirtLearn can take several shapes, and they are not mutually exclusive. This is the
design question the branch exists to answer. Ranked loosely by how well they serve
the "learn by wanting" core:

### 2.1 Visual novel (the leading candidate)
The scene is two characters in a place - the café, the courtyard, a study alcove -
and text advances the conversation. Your dialogue options are limited to the
phrasebook: you can only say what you have learned. A wrong option is not a game
over; it is a sentence your partner gently corrects, and the correction is a new
lesson.

Why it fits: the visual novel is the genre of *almost* - the held line, the pause,
the two beats of silence before someone answers. That pacing is exactly what a
language learner needs, and exactly where erotic tension lives. The slow advance
of a relationship maps one-to-one onto the slow advance of vocabulary.

### 2.2 Phrasebook-as-UI (the core mechanic, works in any style)
There is no dialogue tree. There is a phrasebook. The options you see ARE the words
you know. This is the single rule that makes the game honest:

- You cannot say "te quiero" until you have learned it, and you learn it by needing
  it - by reaching for it in a scene and finding it isn't there yet, then studying,
  then coming back.
- A phrasebook entry carries its own little history: where you learned it, who said
  it to you, how it made you feel. Re-reading your phrasebook is re-living the
  semester.
- The player's language level and the relationship's level rise together. There is
  no separate "gameplay" and "dating." There is one thing.

### 2.3 The messenger layer (study between scenes)
Between dates, you exchange messages with your partner - short texts in the target
language, from a small pool. You pick a reply from your phrasebook; they answer;
their answer teaches you one new phrase. It is a slow-burn layer that does the
language teaching that scenes cannot, and it makes the relationship feel continuous
instead of episodic.

### 2.4 Pronunciation practice (optional, reuses the singing engine)
The pitch-analysis engine from the main game can grade spoken lines: the stress of
"¿cómo estás?", the rolled r. Player speaks into the mic; the game shows the target
contour and yours. Optional - the game works fully on a controller - but for the
player who wants it, this is where "learning a language" stops being metaphorical.

### 2.5 The walk (optional, exploration-light)
A simple movement layer over the Exchange: wander the courtyard, sit in the café,
overhear other students' conversations (which feed you new phrases as ambient
learning). This is P9's camera, reused. It is a spice, not the meal.

**Lean:** visual novel core, phrasebook-as-UI throughout, messenger layer between
scenes, pronunciation optional, walk as garnish. This maximizes the theme and
reuses the existing engine.

## 3. The loop: flirting is the study

1. **You are in class, and the class is a date.** Every scene is a lesson disguised
   as a meeting. The lesson plan is the relationship: meeting, small talk, a real
   question, a confession, the first kiss, and whatever comes after.
2. **Output practice is flirting.** Saying "me gustas mucho" to someone who is
   watching your grammar is the highest-stakes language practice that exists. Your
   voice shakes. The shake is not a failure; it is evidence. Being bad at a new
   language is lovable, not embarrassing. You will mispronounce "llamo." They will
   find it endearing, and they will say it back slowly so you can feel the right
   shape.
3. **Your partner teaches with their body.** When you guess wrong, they correct you
   - "actually you'd say 'te quiero' to someone you love; 'me gustas' is more
   like... here, watch my mouth" - and the correction becomes a permanent phrase in
   your book. Your romance is literally your textbook.
4. **Semesters are distances.** Each stage of the relationship is a semester with
   its vocabulary set. You cannot skip ahead by grinding drills; you advance by
   using the language to move the relationship closer. Fluency and intimacy are the
   same stat.
5. **The last exam is asking.** The final semester's assignment is to ask for what
   you want, in their language, looking at them. The hardest sentence in the game
   is also the most useful one in the real world.

## 4. The characters: aliens among humans, and you pick

The roster is aliens and humans together - the Exchange mixes them on purpose. You
pick one character to play. Each has a species, a target language, and one person
they want to say something to.

### 4.1 Player characters (working roster)

- **A Havari** studying Spanish. Their own language is tonal - their whole skin
  hears - so a language made of lips and breath is a bodily novelty. They want to
  say "buenas noches" back to the person who said it to them and made the word feel
  like a room.
- **A human** studying Japanese. A dock kid who fell for a Neth whose sleeve weaves
  characters as she speaks. They want to learn to read the sleeve, but first they
  want to learn to say "stay" without it sounding like an order.
- **A Chirr** studying French. They want the precise vocabulary for every shade of
  wanting - the word for the back of the neck, the word for the heat behind the
  ears. Also someone. Mostly the words.
- **An Ember** studying Russian. Their light-pigments say what their mouth cannot
  yet; they need the words to match the glow. They want to be read correctly, by
  one particular pair of eyes.
- **A Pyre** studying Mandarin. The tones are half the game - a language that is
  melody and breath - and they want to be inside a conversation the way they would
  be inside a warm wind.
- **A Borei** studying Spanish. Their climate suit turns feeling into weather; they
  want to say the words bare-skinned, in the open air, and it scares them a little.

The game never separates "the aliens" from "the humans." They are all just
students, faces on the same screen.

### 4.2 The partners (the crushes, the teachers)

- **A human from Earth** - fluent, patient, charmed by your mistakes. They repeat
  phrases slowly so you can watch their mouth. They know what they are doing, and
  they want you to keep up.
- **A Havari** learning your language at the same time - so you are each other's
  teacher. When they correct your tone, they reach out and touch your throat to
  show you where the sound lives, and neither of you mentions that the touch
  lingered.
- **A Vessk** - rigorous, delighted, composed. They correct your grammar with the
  authority of someone whose family history is engraved on their shell. When they
  approve of you, they lower their guard plates - a small armored gesture that
  means "here is skin."
- **A Neth** whose sleeve does half the flirting while their voice stays calm. You
  learn to read the sleeve because you want to know what they are actually saying.
  The day you read it correctly, their whole garment changes color, slowly. You
  just made a Neth blush in fabric.
- **An Umlou** who speaks slowly and warmly, and makes you slow down too, until
  your whole conversation is one long unhurried stroke. You keep finding reasons to
  stay in their warmth.
- **A Chirr** who learned three pickup lines before the word for "hello," and is
  proud of this, and delivers each one at full speed with their hands on your
  shoulders.
- **A Pyre** who shivers their whole body of feathers when you get a tone right,
  then looks away and pretends they did not.

Partners are not prizes. They have their own reasons for being there, their own
mistakes (they are also learning), their own arcs, their own hands. The romance is
a duet of two beginners, and that is what keeps it tender.

## 5. The teacher-student theme

Explored in both directions, because at the Exchange everyone is both:

- Sometimes your partner is ahead of you, and their teaching is a kind of courtship
  - the lean in to correct you, the hand on your wrist to slow your accent.
- Sometimes you are ahead - your partner is learning your language, and you get to
  be the teacher, which is its own trust: being handed someone's first words.
- Sometimes the "teacher" is a real teacher, a faculty member who is also a student
  of another language.
- The theme's question, asked plainly: what does it mean to teach someone? What
  does it mean to be taught? When you are falling in love with the person teaching
  you, which of you is really learning?

The power dynamic stays honest because the utopia makes it honest: the teacher is
never an authority exploiting a student. The teacher is a peer who is ahead, and
who is also, somewhere, a beginner. The faculty couple in §7 are the living proof.

## 6. Real languages, real words

Per DESIGN_P8: no magic, everything real. The game uses actual human languages with
actual words. Spanish first, complete; the roster sketches French, Japanese,
Russian, Mandarin. The curriculum is real, and its units are things you would say
to someone you want:

- **Semester 1 (meeting):** greetings, names, "where are you from," one genuine
  compliment, the word for *again*.
- **Semester 2 (small talk):** likes and dislikes, plans, "want to grab a drink?",
  the word for *tonight*.
- **Semester 3 (something real):** "I keep thinking about you," the difference
  between *te quiero* and *te amo*, how to apologize, the word for *stay*.
- **Semester 4 (the brave part):** asking for what you want, in the language you
  have been learning all along, while looking at them.

The player genuinely learns these words. That is not a side effect; it is the game.
(If it teaches you to say something true to someone in Spanish, it has done its job
in the real world, which is the most utopian thing a game can do.)

## 7. The Exchange, the place (shared with P9's Collegium)

Same world as DESIGN_P9: Meridian, the Collegium, the districts, the flare-shutters,
the nine peoples. The Language Exchange is a wing of the Collegium built as a
garden of small rooms - study alcoves, language cafés, a large open courtyard where
paired students practice out loud and everyone pretends not to listen (everyone
listens; it is the Exchange's favorite sport).

- **The language café:** where partners meet. Menus in six scripts; the barista (a
  Chirr) knows which table is learning what and keeps the coffee coming. The tables
  are just far enough apart that a whispered phrase belongs to only two people.
- **The phrase wall:** a wall where students write the best line they learned that
  week in their target language, and everyone translates it into their own. It is
  covered, floor to ceiling, in tiny love letters in every language in the city.
  Once a semester, the curators read the anonymous best ones aloud at the term's
  end, and the courtyard goes quiet, and someone is always crying a little.
- **The faculty room:** three humans, two Havari, one Vessk, and a Neth who argues
  in their sleeve. Against the odds, a happy family - and two of them are, against
  the odds, a couple, who still practice each other's languages over coffee every
  morning. Their love is the living proof that the method works.

## 8. The erotic register (the line, plainly)

Erotic in the broad sense: desire, skin, warmth, kissing, the whole body's wanting.
Never pornographic. The rules are simple:

1. **The most charged moment is the one before the kiss.** The game gives the
   approach full weight and the act a closed door. The fade is a promise, not a
   censure.
2. **Skin is everywhere; sex is off-screen.** Bare necks, hands at wrists, the
   slow removal of a climate suit, the first touch of a Havari's luminescent skin,
   a Chirr's many hands at once, the heat of an Umlou, the shiver of a Pyre's
   feathers. The camera cuts at the bedroom door, every time.
3. **Consent is the mechanic.** Asking - in your target language - is the game.
   "¿Puedo besarte?" is the hardest sentence in the game, and the answer is always
   a person, never a script.
4. **Alien bodies have their own sense of intimacy.** A Havari feels a kiss in
   their skin as much as their lips. A Borei's bare skin is the whole intimacy. A
   Vessk's armor lowering is the undressing. The game is richer because every body
   wants differently (DESIGN_P9 §4).
5. **No one is a conquest.** Every partner wants back, has their own arc, and can
   say no - and a no is honored as completely as a yes. This is the utopia: even
   the desire is kind.

## 9. Open questions

1. **Which gameplay styles to ship?** Visual novel + phrasebook + messenger is the
   lean. Pronunciation and the walk are optional. Confirm the shape.
2. **Which languages, how many?** Start with Spanish, complete, then add.
3. **The teacher-student beat:** how explicit should the faculty-student romance
   be? (Lean: the romance is peer-to-peer; the faculty member's arc is being a
   beginner at another language.)
4. **Replayability:** distinct stories per pairing, or one story with an
   interchangeable cast? (Lean: distinct - the universe's anthology nature.)
5. **The fade line:** confirm the camera always cuts at the bedroom door.
6. **How much of the game is skin versus speech?** (Lean: both, alternating - the
   rhythm of a real conversation.)
