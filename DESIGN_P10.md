# RESONANCE - DESIGN_P10: the mini-game - FlirtLearn, or the courtship method

> "Nobody has ever failed to learn a language they wanted to say something in.
> And nobody has ever learned to say something better than the thing they
> wanted, badly, with their whole skin."

Frame: the second mini-game (UNIVERSE.md §6). P9 is the singing theme. **This one
is the courtship method** - a language-learning game where you are a student at
the Collegium learning a *human* language, and **desire is the curriculum.** You
learn Spanish (or French, or any of them) because there is someone you want to be
able to say something to. The cast is aliens and humans together; you pick your
character, you pick your target language, and you learn it with your pulse.

It is warm, funny, and steeped in wanting. It is erotic in the broadest, oldest
sense: desire, skin, breath, kissing, the unbearable tension of almost, the way
learning someone's language is the most intimate thing you can do with a body.

Tone rule: **as erotic as the law allows - and never pornographic.** Eros lives
in the *almost*. We show skin and heat and hunger and the first brush of a mouth;
we leave the rest to close the door. The game is a kiss you remember, not an
encyclopedia of it.

---

## 1. The premise: the courtship method

The Collegium's School of Voice teaches Havari to humans through song (P9). Its
sibling - the **Language Exchange** - does the reverse and wider: it teaches human
languages to anyone, and it uses the oldest trick in teaching: **motive.** You
don't memorize; you *want*. You learn the word because you need it *tonight*.

- Officially the Exchange calls it "motivational immersion." The students call it
  **FlirtLearn**, and the name is not a joke - it is the method.
- Every student gets a **partner** (a native speaker, or a more advanced student,
  of their target language) and the assignment is the same for everyone: *ask that
  person out in your target language. All semester.* The grammar, the vocabulary,
  the pronunciation - all of it is built out of that one assignment. The exam is
  a date.
- It works. It works so well that the Exchange has the highest fluency rate in
  the Collegium, and the faculty discuss this with the exact air of people who
  accidentally invented fire and are now responsible for it.
- **Why it works, in-universe:** because a language is a body you learn to move
  in. You cannot say "I want you" in a new mouth without feeling the shape of the
  want. The Exchange's founders understood that fluency is a kind of *touching* -
  you practice on someone's ear, their patience, their own trembling fluency.
  Desire is not a distraction from learning. Desire *is* the fastest learner in
  the room.

## 2. The characters: aliens among humans, and you pick

The roster is **aliens and humans together** - the Exchange mixes them on purpose.
You choose one character to play. Each has a home species, a target language, a
personality, and one person they ache to say something to.

### 2.1 The player characters (working roster)

- **A Havari** studying Spanish. Their own language is tonal - their *whole skin*
  hears, so learning a language of lips and breath is a bodily revelation. They
  are learning Spanish because someone on the Exchange said "buenas noches" and
  the *stress* of it - that little fall into the last syllable - rearranged them.
  They want to say that word back to them, in the dark, and mean it.
- **A human** studying Japanese. A dock kid who fell for a Neth whose sleeve
  weaves characters as she speaks - and who wants to learn to read the sleeve,
  but first wants to learn how to say "stay" in a way that doesn't sound like an
  order.
- **A Chirr** studying French. They want the precise, exquisite vocabulary for
  *every* shade of wanting - the way French has a word for the back of the neck,
  and a word for the heat behind the ears. Also, someone. Mostly the words.
- **An Ember** studying Russian. Their light-pigments say what their mouth can't
  yet; they need the words to match the glow. They are learning because they
  want to be *read* correctly, finally - by one particular pair of eyes.
- **A Pyre** studying Mandarin. They learned the tones are the whole game - a
  language that is half melody, half breath - and they want to be inside a
  conversation the way they'd be inside a warm wind. They want to say one thing
  in it, to one person, and have it land.
- **A Borei** studying Spanish. Their climate suit translates everything they
  feel into weather; they want to not need the suit. They are learning to say the
  words bare-skinned, out in the open air, and it terrifies and thrills them.

Each character is a *choice* among human and alien faces on the same screen - the
game never separates "the aliens" from "the humans." They are just students. The
player can be any of them. And every one of them wants something.

### 2.2 The partners (the crushes, the "teachers")

Every player character has a partner - simultaneously the object of the
assignment, the conversation partner, and the de-facto tutor. Drawn from the same
mixed roster:

- **A human from Earth** - fluent, patient, charmed by your mistakes, and
  *deliberately* slow when they repeat a phrase for you, so you can watch their
  mouth. They are teaching you, and they know exactly what they are doing, and
  they want you to keep up.
- **A Havari** learning *your* language at the same time - so you are each
  other's teacher. You both butcher the same sentence and laugh, and when they
  correct your tone they reach out and touch your throat to show you where the
  sound lives, and neither of you mentions that the touch lingered.
- **A Vessk** - rigorous, delighted, and composed. They correct your grammar with
  the gentle authority of someone whose family history is engraved on their
  shell. When they approve of you they *lower their guard plates* - a small,
  armorless gesture that means "here is skin," and it undoes you every time.
- **A Neth** whose sleeve is doing half the flirting - while their voice stays
  devastatingly calm. You learn to read their sleeve because you want to know
  what they're *actually* saying. The day you read it correctly, they don't say
  a word; their whole garment changes color, slowly, and you realize you just
  made a Neth blush in fabric.
- **An Umlou** who speaks slowly, warmly, and makes you slow down too - until
  your whole conversation is a long, unhurried stroke. Their warmth is a *room*;
  you keep finding excuses to stay in it.
- **A Chirr** who learned three pickup lines before the word for "hello," and is
  extremely proud of this, and delivers each one at full speed with their hands
  on your shoulders like a landing.
- **A Pyre** who preens when you get a tone right - a full-body shiver of
  feathers - and then looks away, pretending they didn't, while you die a
  little.

Partners are not prizes. They have their own reasons, their own mistakes (they
are also learning!), their own arcs, and their own hands. The game's romance is a
**duet of two beginners**, and that is what keeps it tender.

## 3. The mechanics: flirting IS the study

The loop is simple and it never lets you forget you are in class - or that the
class is a body:

1. **You have a phrasebook, not a dialogue tree.** Your available lines are the
   words you have actually learned. A beginner has "hola," "¿cómo estás?," "me
   llamo..." and one very hopeful "¿te gusta...?" Everything else is closed until
   you learn it. **Your language level and your love level rise together** -
   because the way to get the next line is to be brave enough to use the current
   one. The word for "lips" is a level above "hello," and you want it that way.
2. **Flirting is output practice.** Saying "me gustas mucho" in real time - to a
   real person who is watching your grammar - is the highest-stakes language
   practice there is. Your voice shakes; the shake is not a failure, it is the
   *evidence*. The game is built to make being bad at a new language *lovable*,
   not embarrassing. You will mispronounce "llamo." They will find it
   endearing - and they will say it back, slowly, so you can feel the right
   shape of it.
3. **Your partner teaches with their body.** When you guess wrong, they correct
   you - gently, with the real-world explanation ("actually you'd say 'te quiero'
   to someone you love, 'me gustas' is more like... here, watch my mouth") - and
   every correction becomes a permanent phrase in your book. **Your romance is
   literally your textbook.** The word you learned the night you touched hands
   is the word you will never forget, because the memory is in your skin.
4. **Levels are semesters, and semesters are distances.** Each tier of the
   relationship - meeting, small talk, a real question, a confession, the first
   kiss, what happens after - is a "semester" with its vocabulary set. You cannot
   skip ahead by grinding drills; you advance by using the language to move the
   relationship closer. **Fluency and intimacy are the same stat.** The last
   semester's exam is asking for what you want, in their language, looking at
   them.
5. **The teacher-student theme is explored in both directions.** Sometimes your
   partner is ahead of you and you are the student - and their teaching is a
   *kind of courtship*: the way they lean in to correct you, the way their hand
   lands on your wrist to slow your accent. Sometimes *you* are ahead - your
   partner is learning your language, and you get to be the teacher, which is
   its own tenderness: to be trusted with someone's first words. And sometimes
   the "teacher" is an actual teacher - a faculty member who is also a student
   of another language, because at the Exchange everyone is both. The game
   deliberately asks: **what does it mean to teach someone something? What does
   it mean to be taught? And when you are falling in love with the person
   teaching you, which of you is really learning?**

## 4. Real languages, real words, real mouths

Per DESIGN_P8's rule - no magic, everything real - FlirtLearn uses **actual human
languages with actual words.** Spanish is the default (your own example); the
roster sketches French, Japanese, Russian, and Mandarin. The phrasebook is a real
curriculum, and its units are *things you'd say to someone you want*:

- **Semester 1 (meeting):** greetings, names, "where are you from," one genuine
  compliment, the word for *again*.
- **Semester 2 (small talk):** likes and dislikes, plans, "want to grab a
  drink?", the word for *tonight*.
- **Semester 3 (something real):** "I keep thinking about you," the difference
  between *te quiero* and *te amo*, how to apologize, the word for *stay*.
- **Semester 4 (the brave part):** asking for what you want. In the language
  you've been learning all along. While looking at them.

The player genuinely learns these words - the game is a cozy, romantic language
primer wrapped in a love story, and it *is* the love story. (If it teaches you to
say something true to someone in Spanish, it has done its job in the real world,
which is the most utopian thing a game can do.)

## 5. The erotic craft laws (this branch's constitution)

The game is as erotic as the law allows, and never pornographic. The rules:

1. **Eros lives in the almost.** The most charged moment in the game is the one
   before the kiss - the held breath, the slow lean, the "may I?" said in a
   language you just learned. We give the *tension* full weight and the *act*
   its closing door. The game is a long, delicious almost.
2. **Skin is everywhere; sex is off-screen.** Bodies are bare, warm, touched,
   tasted, kissed. We describe skin the way the weather describes light. But the
   game cuts away at the threshold of the bedroom door - with a frame that is
   itself romantic, so the fade is a promise, not a censure.
3. **Desire has a thousand shapes.** Kissing, yes - but also: the back of a
   neck, a hand at a wrist, a shared breath, the sound of someone's name in
   another mouth, the weight of a garment laid down, the slow removal of a
   climate suit, the first bare touch of a Havari's luminescent skin, a Chirr's
   many hands at once, the *heat* of an Umlou, the shiver of a Pyre's feathers.
   The game's eroticism is a *catalog of skins*, not a single scene repeated.
4. **Consent is erotic.** Asking - in your target language - is the mechanic.
   "¿Puedo besarte?" is the hardest and best sentence in the game, and the
   answer is always a person, never a script. The game's romance is the
   *practice of asking*, which is the most grown-up and most thrilling thing
   there is.
5. **Alien bodies are not human bodies with hats.** Eros must respect the
   species' own sense of skin, heat, and intimacy (DESIGN_P9 §4). A Havari's
   pleasure is in resonance - they feel a kiss in their skin as much as their
   lips. A Borei's bare skin is the *whole* intimacy. A Vessk's armor lowering
   is the undressing. The game is richer because every body wants differently.
6. **No one is a conquest.** Every partner wants back, has their own arc, and
   can say no - and the game honors a no as completely as a yes. (This is the
   utopia: even the desire is kind.)

## 6. The Exchange, the place (shared with P9's Collegium)

Same world as DESIGN_P9: Meridian, the Collegium, the districts, the
flare-shutters, the nine peoples. The Language Exchange is a wing of the
Collegium built as a **garden of small rooms** - study alcoves, language cafés, a
huge open courtyard where paired students practice out loud and everyone pretends
not to listen (everyone listens; it is the Exchange's favorite sport).

- **The language café:** where partners meet. Menus in six scripts; the barista
  (a Chirr) knows which table is learning what and keeps the coffee coming. The
  tables are just far enough apart that a whispered phrase belongs to only two
  people, and the café is, on purpose, the best-smelling place in the city.
- **The phrase wall:** a wall where students write the best line they learned
  that week in their target language, and everyone else translates it into their
  own. It is covered, floor to ceiling, in tiny love letters in every language
  in the city - and once a semester, the wall's curators (two humans and a
  Havari) read aloud the anonymous best ones at the term's end, and the whole
  courtyard falls silent, and someone is always crying a little.
- **The faculty room:** three humans, two Havari, one Vessk, and a Neth who
  argues in their sleeve. They are, against all odds, a happy family - and two
  of them are, against all odds, a couple, who still practice each other's
  languages over coffee every morning. Their love is the Exchange's living
  proof that the method works.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Which languages, how many?** Spanish, French, Japanese, Russian, Mandarin -
   or fewer, done deeper? (Lean: start with one - Spanish - and make it
   *complete*, then add.)
2. **The teacher-student beat:** how explicit should the faculty-student romance
   be? (Lean: the romance is peer-to-peer; the faculty member's arc is *being a
   beginner at another language*, keeping the power honest - see the couple in
   §6.)
3. **Replayability:** distinct stories per pairing, or one story with
   interchangeable cast? (Lean: distinct - the universe's anthology nature.)
4. **How much actual language teaching?** Embedded in the flirt, or full
   lessons? (Lean: embedded - the game teaches by *wanting to say something*.)
5. **The fade line:** where exactly does the camera cut? (Lean: at the bedroom
   door, always, with a frame that is itself a love letter. Confirm this is the
   line you want.)
6. **Sensuality budget:** how much of the game is *skin* (touch, temperature,
   the bareness of different bodies) versus *speech* (the wanting said out
   loud)? (Lean: both, alternating - a rhythm, like a conversation.)
